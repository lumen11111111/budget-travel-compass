# How to Plan a Senior-Friendly City Break

“Senior-friendly” should describe a plan fitted to a traveler, not a city label or an assumption about age. One person may want long walks and late performances; another may prioritize seating, step-free routes or an unhurried morning. Begin with preference and access, then count the effort between attractions.

## Define the traveler's effort budget

Use “energy” as an editorial planning term: how much walking, standing, transferring and decision-making the traveler wants in a day. It is not a medical score. Personal health, medication or mobility advice belongs with qualified professionals and the traveler.

## Choose a compact stay zone

Map the repeated door-to-door journeys: arrival, main interests, evening return and departure. Compare surface, gradients, station access, toilets, seating and the number of changes. A short map distance can still contain stairs or a difficult interchange.

Official operators should control factual access claims. TfL, for example, currently exposes options for least walking, fewer changes and step-free routes, plus access maps, toilet information and status updates. That proves what to look for—not what another city's network provides.

## Plan one anchor and one flexible companion

Place one meaningful anchor in each day, with a nearby meal, short visit or rest option. Avoid scheduling the most demanding route immediately after arrival. State where sitting, toilets and a weather alternative are available, then recheck them close to travel.

## Inspect the full access chain

“Accessible attraction” is incomplete if the pavement, station lift, entrance, queue or return route breaks the chain. Use current transport, attraction and accommodation sources and request direct evidence for individual needs.
