# How to Use AI to Plan a Trip Without Trusting It Blindly

AI can turn a messy set of wishes into a useful planning conversation. It can also produce a confident opening time that never existed. The productive middle ground is not “trust but verify.” It is to give generation and verification different jobs from the start.

Use AI to expand, organize, compare, and challenge a plan. Use official authorities and direct providers to establish the facts that can cost money, invalidate a journey, or change your safety.

## Write the trip brief before the prompt

A weak request—“plan five days in Italy”—invites generic output and hidden assumptions. Begin with constraints you know:

- travelers and relevant access needs;
- dates and usable arrival/departure hours;
- fixed bookings or events;
- budget band and what it includes;
- preferred pace and transport tolerance;
- interests, dislikes, and non-negotiable rest;
- facts that are unknown and must be researched.

Do not add passport numbers, booking references, health records, home address, financial details, or confidential work information. A tool cannot organize data you did not give it, but giving it more personal data is not automatically better planning.

## Assign AI the low-authority jobs

Good planning tasks are reversible and inspectable. Ask for:

- questions the group has not answered;
- three different shapes for the trip, with stated trade-offs;
- geographic grouping of a list you already supplied;
- a table comparing options using your criteria;
- missing dependencies and research tasks;
- a concise summary of your own notes.

Ask the tool to label assumptions and unknowns. Require it to distinguish ideas from facts and to avoid inventing citations. This improves the audit trail, but it does not make the output true.

## Keep consequential facts outside the model

Create a verification queue for any claim about:

- passport, visa, entry, customs, or health requirements;
- transport schedules, fares, disruption, or last service;
- opening hours, closure, admission, permits, or accessibility;
- live accommodation and flight prices or availability;
- cancellation, baggage, insurance, or booking policy;
- safety warnings, weather alerts, or emergency instructions.

For each item, identify the responsible source: government or border authority, embassy/consulate where appropriate, transport operator, attraction/land manager, airline, property, insurer, or official warning service. Record the URL and checked date. A search snippet, AI citation, blog, or old screenshot is not the final authority.

Google's current Gemini guidance illustrates why this separation matters. Its Help Center says Gemini can present inaccurate information as factual and tells users to double-check responses. That is a first-party limitation statement, not evidence that every AI product behaves identically or that any fixed error rate applies.

## Compare proposals without outsourcing judgment

AI is useful when the inputs are yours. Give it three accommodations with total prices and cancellation terms you verified, then ask it to compare them against your criteria. Give it operating hours you copied from official pages, then ask it to flag timing conflicts.

Check the transformation. Did it preserve currencies and dates? Did it quietly treat walking time as accessible? Did it rank an option by a value you never chose? Return to the original evidence before making a payment.

The model can reveal a contradiction. It cannot resolve a contradiction between official sources without further research.

## Inspect privacy before sharing the trip

Privacy controls are product-, account-, region-, and setting-specific. Read the current first-party notice for the exact tool and account type. Check whether prompts and uploads are stored, used to improve models, reviewed by people, shared with connected apps, retained after deletion, or governed by an organization administrator.

For a current example, Google's Gemini Apps Privacy Hub describes different behavior based on the Keep Activity setting, selectable auto-delete periods for saved activity, temporary retention when activity is off, human review for some data, and separate handling for connected services. Those details can change and do not transfer to another provider. The practical rule is simpler: minimize what you share, disconnect unneeded integrations, and review settings before uploading documents.

## Run a two-pass workflow

**Pass 1 — Generate.** Ask for possibilities, a proposed structure, open questions, and assumptions.

**Pass 2 — Verify.** Move each consequential claim into a ledger. Replace it with an official fact, mark it unresolved, or remove the dependency from the plan.

Only after verification should you ask AI to format the itinerary. Even then, preserve the links and checked dates so the plan can be refreshed near departure.

## Prompt Brief

```text
Trip frame: [travelers, dates, usable hours, base]
Budget band: [amount, currency, included categories]
Pace and interests: [specific preferences]
Fixed commitments: [verified details]
Known options: [facts I supply]

Your job:
1. Propose three structurally different plans.
2. State assumptions and unresolved questions.
3. Do not invent live prices, schedules, rules, hours, or citations.
4. Put consequential facts in a VERIFY column.
5. Explain trade-offs; do not choose for me.
```

AI has finished its useful work when it gives you better questions and a clearer structure. It has exceeded its authority when its fluency becomes the reason you believe a travel fact.
