# Budget Travel Compass — Editorial Standard v1

Status: **APPROVED EDITORIAL STANDARD V1**  
Approval gate: Pilot Final Review passed on 2026-08-14  
Import state: Not approved for CMS import or publication  
Created: 2026-08-14  
Applies to: research, commissioning, drafting, revision, and pre-publication review  
Does not authorize: CMS import, publication, or production deployment

## 1. Brand Editorial Purpose

Budget Travel Compass publishes practical guides for affordable independent travel. “Affordable” means helping readers understand trade-offs, total cost, and usable value—not promising the lowest price. “Independent” means giving readers enough method and evidence to make their own decision—not withholding important risk or replacing judgment with a rigid rule.

An article should leave the reader with one of three outcomes:

- a decision they can make with greater clarity;
- a process they can execute and verify;
- a practical model they can adapt to their own trip.

General travel inspiration, motivational reassurance, and product discovery are secondary. They cannot substitute for solving the commissioned reader problem.

## 2. Audience

The primary reader plans and books travel independently, is cost-conscious without treating price as the only value, and may have limited experience with the specific decision. Do not assume the reader:

- is American, British, or from any single country;
- uses a particular currency, payment method, passport, airline, or booking platform;
- is young, able-bodied, digitally fluent, or traveling alone;
- wants maximum activity density or minimum comfort;
- understands travel-industry terminology.

Explain the decision without patronizing the reader. Define a term when misunderstanding it could change cost, eligibility, or risk.

## 3. Voice

The brand voice is clear, restrained, evidence-disciplined, practical, and honest about uncertainty.

Use:

- concrete nouns and verbs;
- specific trade-offs rather than “pros and cons” filler;
- calm explanations of why a step matters;
- direct acknowledgment of limits, unknowns, and changing information;
- varied sentence rhythm suited to the article’s form.

Avoid:

- false intimacy or invented travel experience;
- lecturing, moralizing, or performing expertise;
- fear-based framing;
- breathless bargain language;
- generic encouragement that does not change the decision;
- long sequences of commands when explanatory prose would improve flow.

Imperatives are useful inside a true procedure, checklist, or instruction. They should not become the default rhythm of every paragraph.

## 4. Search Intent Discipline

Every article starts with an approved commissioning brief that states:

- Primary Query
- Search Intent
- Specific User Problem
- Unique Promise
- What This Article Will Not Cover
- Difference from sibling articles

The draft must fulfill the primary intent before serving adjacent interests. A pillar may point to related decisions, but it cannot absorb every supporting guide.

During review, summarize the article’s answer in one sentence. If the sentence also describes another commissioned article, inspect for cannibalization. Resolve overlap by tightening scope, adding a handoff, or redefining the practical output—not by adding more keywords.

## 5. Article Structure Principles

Structure follows the reader’s problem, not a site-wide outline.

Possible structures include:

- chronological stages for a dependency-driven workflow;
- comparison dimensions for a choice between options;
- a workshop for building a system;
- scenarios for a budget or planning decision;
- evidence layers for verification;
- a myth-and-correction sequence;
- an annotated example followed by adaptation guidance;
- a narrative explanation that culminates in one decision tool.

Sections should do distinct work. If two consecutive sections classify the same material in different ways, compress or combine them. Let the practical core arrive before the reader has processed duplicate conceptual framing.

Introductions should normally tell the reader within 100–150 words:

- what problem the article solves;
- why the obvious answer is incomplete;
- what method, decision, or tool the reader will receive.

Conclusions should close the article’s specific decision. They should not restate every heading or end with generic motivation.

## 6. Structural Variation Requirement

**Structural variation is required.** Articles from the same brand should share standards, not skeletons.

Structure is determined by:

1. Search Intent
2. Reader Problem
3. Evidence Type
4. Practical Utility

Do not default every article to:

> Define Problem → Write Constraints → Verify Official Sources → Build Checklist → Record Decision → Readiness Test

That sequence is appropriate for some high-risk planning workflows. It is not the house template.

At batch review, compare:

- introductions;
- H2 sequences;
- transition patterns;
- practical-tool shapes;
- conclusions;
- repeated generic advice.

Five articles may all be clear and practical while using a search log, dependency checklist, scenario worksheet, packing workshop, and readiness framework respectively. That difference is a quality signal.

## 7. Practical Utility Standard

**Practical does not mean checklist.** Practicality means the reader receives an artifact, method, comparison, or demonstration appropriate to the search intent.

Valid forms include:

- Checklist
- Matrix
- Worksheet
- Formula
- Calculator
- Decision Tree
- Worked Example
- Scenario Comparison
- Annotated Comparison
- Timeline
- Sample Itinerary
- Packing Layout
- Before / After
- Source Hierarchy
- Myth Correction
- Step Sequence
- Verification Flow
- Planning Canvas

Choose the form by asking: “What must the reader do, see, calculate, compare, or decide after reading?”

The utility must be complete enough to use. “Make a checklist” is not a checklist. “Compare the total cost” is not a calculator. A table added only to look practical should be removed.

Original formulas and thresholds must be labeled accurately. Editorial decision aids are not scientific laws. Explain variables, assumptions, and the consequence of changing them.

## 8. Research Tier Rules

### Tier A — High freshness or consequence

Use official and primary sources first for airline rules, baggage, passports, entry, visas, transit, transport operations, safety, insurance wording, platform functions, eSIM/connectivity, accommodation policies, legal requirements, and comparable high-risk claims.

Research must identify jurisdiction, traveler profile, route, date, and policy scope where relevant. A government page written for one nationality does not become a universal rule.

### Tier B — Mixed

Use official data or authorities plus high-quality dated secondary sources for budgets, city breaks, food costs, nature access, long-trip costs, and topics combining stable method with changing examples.

### Tier C — Evergreen

Use reliable expert or primary sources for factual claims, but do not decorate ordinary editorial judgment with unnecessary authority citations. Packing logic, group coordination, or slow-travel principles can be editorial methods. Weather, health, safety, technical performance, and carrier restrictions still require evidence when asserted.

Research tier controls source burden; it does not determine article length or quality.

## 9. Claim-to-Source Rules

Research precedes drafting:

> Production Brief → Claim Planning → Source Research → Claim-to-Source Ledger → Outline Validation → Draft → Claim QA → Editorial QA

Every consequential or freshness-sensitive claim must map to a source in the ledger. The ledger records:

- Claim
- Source and URL
- Source Type
- Publication or Access Date
- Freshness
- Where Used
- Verification Notes and limitations

A source list at the end of an article is not a substitute for claim mapping.

Source strength must match claim strength. A platform help page can verify its own feature. It cannot prove every provider’s policy. A traveler discussion can signal an experience pattern. It cannot be the sole authority for an entry rule or safety claim.

Do not inherit legacy citations merely because the source article used them. Old sources are research leads only.

If a source is unnecessary after revision, remove the citation and ledger row rather than retaining decorative authority. If a factual claim remains, preserve or replace its evidence.

## 10. Freshness Rules

Every article receives HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW freshness and a specific pre-publication recheck list.

Always recheck before publication when the article mentions current:

- prices, fares, taxes, exchange rates, or fees;
- schedules, operating hours, closures, or service availability;
- baggage, fare-family, cancellation, refund, or accommodation rules;
- entry, visa, transit, health, medication, or legal requirements;
- safety advisories, emergency contacts, or local conditions;
- app, platform, carrier, eSIM, or payment functionality;
- insurance wording or exclusions.

Do not promise a fixed update cadence. State what must be rechecked and perform the check at the publication gate.

## 11. Safety / Health / Legal / Identity Sensitivity

High-consequence guidance must be operational without becoming alarmist or falsely reassuring.

- Do not guarantee safety or rank destinations as universally safe.
- Do not publish legal, visa, medical, or insurance advice as universal fact.
- Do not stereotype destinations, cultures, genders, ages, disability, or traveler identities.
- Do not imply that following precautions makes a victim responsible for another person’s harmful behavior.
- Separate official guidance from local experience signals and editorial judgment.
- Identify nationality, jurisdiction, route, identity, and timing limits.
- Offer options and verification methods rather than moral commands.

Prepared readers should feel informed and capable, not constantly threatened. When risk controls accumulate, rebalance the article with the reader’s positive objective: independence, enjoyment, access, confidence based on preparation, or the ability to revise a plan.

Safety, health, legal, accessibility, and identity-related drafts require an additional sensitivity review before publication.

## 12. Commercial / Affiliate Independence

Editorial conclusions are independent of commercial relationships.

- Do not declare a platform, airline, insurer, property, card, app, or product “best” without a defined, researched comparison methodology.
- Do not use affiliate roundups as factual authority.
- Disclose any commercial relationship at the applicable editorial and legal gate.
- Compare a product’s total terms and suitability, not only price or commission availability.
- A first-party source can verify its own policy or feature; it is not independent evidence of superiority.

If no product recommendation is needed to solve the query, do not add one.

## 13. Examples and Fictional Demonstrations

Examples must be labeled by evidence type:

- **Current example:** sourced, dated, and rechecked before publication.
- **Fictional demonstration:** explicitly invented to show arithmetic, structure, or use of a tool; not a quote, average, or recommendation.
- **Experience signal:** anecdotal or community material used to identify questions or patterns, not to prove high-risk facts.

Never present a fictional price as a market norm. State currency, date, route, traveler count, and inclusion assumptions when a real price is used.

Worked arithmetic must be independently recalculated during review. Tables must remain comprehensible on mobile.

## 14. Internal Linking

Internal links follow the architecture:

- Pillar to specific supporting decision
- Supporting guide back to the relevant pillar
- Sibling links where the reader’s next problem is predictable
- Limited cross-cluster links where one decision changes another

Do not create a live link to an unpublished route. During content preparation, record **Planned Internal Links** only.

Anchor text should name the reader’s next problem. Do not use random “read more” links or repeat links solely for SEO density.

## 15. Metadata

Every production draft includes:

- Production Title
- Slug
- SEO Title
- Meta Description
- Excerpt

Titles should state the topic naturally without keyword stuffing or clickbait. Meta descriptions should usually fit approximately 140–160 characters, but natural, accurate language takes priority over mechanical length. Excerpts serve article cards and must not simply copy the meta description.

Do not rewrite accurate metadata merely because body prose changed. Review it when scope, promise, practical output, or primary query changes.

## 16. Anti-Template Rules

The following are prohibited as batch defaults:

- identical intro formulas;
- repeated H2 sequences;
- one practical checklist in every article;
- the same constraint-setting paragraph across topics;
- generic “verify official sources” blocks copied without topic-specific evidence needs;
- identical readiness conclusions;
- three equal-length paragraphs under every heading;
- uniform word counts detached from reader need.

Batch QA checks exact paragraphs of 120+ characters, exact sentences of 10+ words, intros, conclusions, H2 structures, generic transitions, tool structures, and old template residue.

Shared brand principles may recur as ideas. They must be expressed where relevant and integrated into the topic rather than pasted as standard copy.

## 17. AI-Style Writing Patterns to Avoid

Do not use:

- “In today’s fast-paced world”
- “Whether you’re a seasoned traveler or…”
- “Travel doesn’t have to break the bank”
- “By following these tips…”
- “Ultimately…” as a generic conclusion
- “Remember, the key is…”
- “With a little planning…”

Also avoid:

- rhetorical questions used as filler;
- repeated three-part slogans;
- every paragraph beginning with a command;
- uniform section lengths and transitions;
- fake first-person tests, journeys, expertise, or client stories;
- vague intensifiers such as “game-changing,” “seamless,” or “must-have” without evidence;
- motivational conclusions disconnected from the article’s decision.

AI assistance does not reduce editorial responsibility. Every claim, example, structure, and phrase remains subject to human review.

## 18. Content Review Checklist

### Commission

- [ ] Primary query and search intent are fulfilled.
- [ ] Unique promise is visible in the article, not only metadata.
- [ ] “Does not cover” boundaries are respected.
- [ ] Sibling articles retain their independent intent.

### Structure and voice

- [ ] Structure follows this reader problem rather than a house template.
- [ ] Intro identifies problem, complexity, and output quickly.
- [ ] Sections do distinct work without duplicate classification passes.
- [ ] Sentence rhythm and transitions fit the article form.
- [ ] Conclusion closes the specific decision.
- [ ] No fake expertise, experience, anecdote, or AI-style filler.

### Practical utility

- [ ] Practical form is appropriate to the search intent.
- [ ] Tool, process, example, or comparison is fully implemented.
- [ ] Formula variables and assumptions are explained.
- [ ] Tables/calculations have been tested.

### Claims and sources

- [ ] Consequential claims appear in the ledger.
- [ ] Source type and jurisdiction match the claim.
- [ ] Source URLs are valid, correctly titled, and point to expected pages.
- [ ] Article does not expand beyond source support.
- [ ] Decorative citations have been removed.
- [ ] Current claims have a pre-publication recheck requirement.

### Sensitivity and independence

- [ ] No safety guarantee, stereotype, victim-blaming, or universal legal/health rule.
- [ ] Appropriate sensitivity review completed.
- [ ] Commercial references are necessary and independent.
- [ ] Fictional, current, and experience examples are labeled correctly.

### Batch integrity

- [ ] No duplicated long paragraph or sentence.
- [ ] Intro, H2 sequence, practical form, and conclusion are structurally distinct.
- [ ] Planned internal links do not create unpublished production routes.
- [ ] Metadata is accurate and complete.

## 19. Pre-Publication Freshness Gate

Research completion is not publication approval. Immediately before controlled publication:

1. Open every HIGH-freshness source used in the draft.
2. Confirm the URL, page title, responsible authority, update date, jurisdiction, and current claim language.
3. Recheck all named prices, fares, fees, schedules, policies, platform functions, entry/transit rules, safety advice, emergency contacts, and insurance wording.
4. Remove or rewrite any claim that cannot be verified at the required authority level.
5. Recalculate formulas and worked examples after any value changes.
6. Re-run cannibalization, sensitivity, metadata, and cross-article duplication checks.
7. Confirm the target route and planned internal links exist before adding live links.
8. Record human editorial approval separately from research status.

Only the authorized human Import Gate may advance reviewed content into CMS preparation. `READY_FOR_FINAL_PILOT_REVIEW` does not mean approved for import or publication.
